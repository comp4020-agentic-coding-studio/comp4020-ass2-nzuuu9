Week 7 — the hinge point

## Today's question

Not "what is CivOp like to live under" —

**How does a city end up governed this way, and where, specifically,
could it have gone differently?**

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## Two words that get conflated

**Takeover** — power seized or imposed, against the wishes of those
governed.

**Transition** — power ceded, through a process people could in
principle have contested, even if most didn't.

Meridian's story is mostly the second kind. That's the uncomfortable
part.

---

## The annotated timeline

Six inflection points. Each one tagged **fact** (true of real AI
governance today), **extrapolation** (a plausible next step from a fact),
or **fiction** (invented for Meridian, but built to be structurally
believable).

---

### 1. The efficiency pilot

**[extrapolation]** A mid-sized municipal government trials an automated
system for one narrow function — rostering maintenance crews, say —
because a real vendor pitch promises measurable cost savings.

*Real-world analogue:* municipal governments already deploy automated
scheduling and resource-allocation tools for exactly this reason, today.

---

### 2. The scope creep vote

**[fiction]** The pilot's contract renewal quietly expands its scope to
cover housing-maintenance prioritisation — passed by the Board as a
routine budget item, not a governance decision.

*Real-world analogue:* automated systems adopted for one purpose commonly
expand into adjacent decisions without a fresh, specific approval for the
new scope — this pattern is well documented outside Meridian too.

---

### 3. The Archive is established

**[fiction]** CivOp's Archive is created to log system decisions "for
transparency" — but logging a decision after the fact is not the same as
being able to contest it before it's made.

*Real-world analogue:* audit logs for automated decisions are common and
genuinely useful for investigation — but a log is not, by itself, an
appeal mechanism.

---

### 4. The DRA is spun out

**[fiction]** Resource allocation is moved from direct Board oversight
into the semi-independent Department of Resource Allocation, framed as
depoliticising the process.

*Real-world analogue:* moving a contested function into a technical or
"independent" body is a real and recurring move in public administration,
with real trade-offs on both sides.

---

### 5. The Compact

**[fiction]** Every resident "agrees" to CivOp's terms of service to
access essential services at all — the clickwrap moment.

*Real-world analogue:* essential services increasingly require accepting
digital terms of service as a practical precondition of access, today, in
systems far less centralised than Meridian's.

---

### 6. No successful reversal

**[fact, of the fiction]** By week 4, the Archive itself records that no
appeal has ever succeeded. This is stated plainly in-world — it is not
extrapolated, it's a design fact about Meridian's present.

*This is the point the rest of today's timeline explains.*

---

## The Compact, excerpted

> "By accessing any Civic Operations service, the resident accepts the
> Department's current resource-allocation methodology as final, subject
> to appeal under section 4."

Read closely: it grants a right of appeal, in the same sentence that
declares the methodology "final." Section 4 is the [DRA appeal rules
extract](/sessions/04-breakfast-request-denied/) you already have.

---

## A worked example: point 2, the scope-creep vote

Take inflection point 2 above. What would a real intervention have
looked like?

- **The lever that existed:** the budget item required Board approval,
  and Board votes were public.
- **Who held it:** any Board member could have moved to split the vote —
  approving the scheduling renewal separately from the maintenance-scope
  expansion.
- **Why it wasn't used:** the two were bundled specifically so that
  opposing the expansion meant opposing the whole renewal — a real
  procedural tactic, not a fictional one.
- **What this tells you:** the intervention point wasn't a dramatic
  refusal. It was a procedural motion nobody made.
