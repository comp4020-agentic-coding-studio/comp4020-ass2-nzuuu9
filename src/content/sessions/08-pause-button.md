**Monday 26 April 2027 — story time: present day (real world).**

## This week's problem

Week 2 mapped who holds power over CivOp, in fiction. This week asks the
same question about a system that actually exists today, governed by
rules that are still being written. Meridian's Board could suspend an
allocation rule by majority vote; the real question this week is what the
equivalent lever looks like for a system you could point to right now.

## Learning goals

By the end of this session you should be able to:

- Name the levers currently available for governing a deployed AI
  system — pausing its use, restricting its scope, staging a wider rollout,
  or letting it proceed unrestricted — and what evidence typically
  triggers each.
- Compare a fictional governance structure against a real one without
  collapsing the two, or assuming the real one is either better or worse
  by default.

## Preparation and materials

Read, before the session:

- The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) —
  a real, publicly issued framework for identifying and responding to risk
  in deployed AI systems, used here for its structure (govern, map,
  measure, manage), not as the only framework that exists.
- [Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropics-responsible-scaling-policy) —
  a real, published example of an organisation committing in advance to
  specific capability thresholds that trigger specific governance
  responses, which is the closest real-world equivalent to a "pause
  button" you'll find this week.

## Class activity

In pairs, pick one real, currently-deployed AI system — a content
recommendation system, an automated eligibility check, a hiring-screening
tool, anything you can name and describe concretely. Using the two
sources above as reference points, work out: which lever currently applies
to it (pause, restrict, stage, or proceed), what evidence would have to
appear to move it to the next lever up, and who — specifically, by role
or institution, not "someone" — would actually have to act on that
evidence. Then place your system's dependency chain next to your week 2
diagram of CivOp: where are the two alike, and where does the comparison
break down?

## Expected output

A short governance-lever brief for the system you chose: current lever,
trigger for the next one, and the specific actor who'd have to act — plus
two or three sentences on how it compares to week 2's CivOp map. This is
real-world grounding for A3's requirement to defend a concrete direction
for present-day AI governance, not fiction.
